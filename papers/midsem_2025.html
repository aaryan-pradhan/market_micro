2 · Deep-dive concept summary

2.1 What liquidity is. Harris: liquidity is the ability to trade large size, quickly, at low cost, when you want to trade. That sentence packs in four separate questions a trader asks:

Trader asksDimension
How fast can I trade?Immediacy
How much does it cost me?Width
How much can I trade at that cost?Depth
How fast does the price come back after I trade?Resiliency

Liquidity is two-sided:

  • Supply: standing limit orders and dealer quotes offer other traders a chance to trade (Orders deck p2).
  • Demand: market orders and marketable limit orders take that chance (Intro p23: "Demands immediacy").

Terms used below (Intro p14; Liquidity p9):

  • Ask \(P_A\) = the lowest price a seller will accept. Bid \(P_B\) = the highest price a buyer will pay.
  • Mid price \(P_M = \tfrac12(P_A + P_B)\).
  • \(Q_A\) and \(Q_B\) = the number of shares offered at the best ask and at the best bid.

2.2 The four dimensions.

(1) Immediacy: how quickly a trade of a given size can be done at a given cost.

  • Supplied by: dealers and standing limit orders. Demanded by: market orders.
  • Measured by: time to execution; how often trades happen; whether a counterparty exists right now.
  • Example: a stock with an active limit order book fills a 100-share market order in milliseconds. In an illiquid SME stock, a buyer may wait hours for a seller. NSE runs a separate call auction for illiquid stocks (Intro p29) because continuous trading there has no immediacy.
  • Impact on the market: patient traders get better prices, impatient ones pay for speed. Stoll 2000 defines friction as exactly this: the price concession paid for immediacy (Liquidity p30).

(2) Width (also called tightness): the cost of a trade of a given size, per share.

  • Measured by the spread (Liquidity p9):
    • Quoted spread QSPR = \(P_A - P_B\)
    • Proportional quoted spread PQSPR = \((P_A - P_B)/P_M\)
    • Effective spread ESPR = \(2\lvert P_t - P_M\rvert\), which captures trades that happen inside the quotes
  • Example (Axis Bank, Intro p20): bid 664.40, ask 664.55.
    • QSPR = 0.15, and PQSPR = 0.15/664.475 = 0.0226%.
    • A round trip (buy then sell) costs one full spread.
  • Impact on the market: a wide spread raises the cost of trading, so people trade less. It also raises the cost of capital (Biais–Glosten–Spatt, p4: 5 cents × 360 billion shares = $18 billion). Amihud & Mendelson (1986) use the spread as their illiquidity proxy (Liquidity p3).

(3) Depth: the size of trade that can be done at a given price or cost.

  • Measured by: DEP = \(\tfrac12(Q_A + Q_B)\), in shares (Liquidity p9), or the full volume profile of the book (Intro p15).
  • Example (Axis Bank):
    • DEP = ½(453 + 714) = 583.5 shares at the best quotes.
    • A 2,000-share market buy takes all 714 shares at 664.55, then 1,286 shares at 664.60. Its average price is 664.582.
    • So it pays 0.107 per share over the mid, compared with 0.075 (half the spread) for a small order. The book is shallow, so the order walked the book, and the best ask moves up to 664.60.
  • Impact on the market: depth decides the price impact of large orders, which matters for institutions and block trades. Width and depth move together: in Chordia et al., QSPR and DEP have correlation −0.396 (Liquidity p10). When the spread widens, depth thins, so the two dimensions reinforce each other.

(4) Resiliency: how quickly prices return to their fundamental level after a large uninformed trade pushes them away.

  • Supplied by: traders who post new limit orders after the price moves (Intro p23: a market-if-touched order "supplies resiliency"). Value traders also step in against mispricing.
  • Measured by: how fast the price reverts, and how fast the book refills.
  • Example: after the Axis Bank buy above, the ask sits at 664.60. In a resilient market, new sellers post at 664.55 within seconds, and the spread and depth recover.
  • Impact on the market: resiliency separates transitory volatility (moves that revert, caused by uninformed traders) from fundamental volatility (Volatility deck p10–11). Low resiliency lets uninformed trades leave lasting price errors, which hurts price discovery.

2.3 How the four fit together.

The dimensions trade off against each other. Faster trading (immediacy) or larger size (depth) costs more (width). Chordia et al. and Amihud (2002) are simply measurements of these same dimensions.

2.4 The monopolistic market maker (Garman 1976; Inventory deck p4–5, p19–23).

Setup:

  • One dealer sets the prices: an ask \(p_a\) for public buys and a bid \(p_b\) for public sells.
  • Every order is for one unit, and the dealer's goal is maximum profit per unit time.
  • Buy orders and sell orders arrive asynchronously, as independent Poisson processes with rates \(\lambda_a(p_a)\) and \(\lambda_b(p_b)\).
  • The dealer cannot borrow cash or stock. Running out of either is failure, which Garman analyses as a gambler's ruin problem (p17–18).

The key point: buyers and sellers almost never show up at the same instant.

  • Without the dealer, a buyer would have to wait for a seller.
  • The dealer bridges that time gap from his own inventory. In the dealership-vs-auction table (p22), orders are "filled against the market-maker's inventory" and the transaction stream is "Poisson due to immediate execution."
  • So what the monopolist sells is immediacy, and the spread is its price.
  • He can keep selling it only while his inventory of cash and stock lasts. That is why Garman needs \(\lambda_a p_a > \lambda_b p_b\) and zero drift, \(\lambda_a = \lambda_b\) (p19).

3 · Comprehensive solution

Part (a) [4 marks]: the four dimensions.

Definition. Liquidity is the ability to trade a large size, quickly, at low cost, when one wants to trade (Harris). It has four dimensions.

  1. Immediacy: how quickly a trade of a given size can be executed.
    • Example: a market order in a liquid large-cap stock fills instantly. An illiquid SME stock may need a call auction, as NSE runs for illiquid stocks.
    • Impact: impatient traders can trade when they need to, and this is what they pay the spread for (Demsetz 1968: friction is the price of immediacy).
  2. Width (tightness): the cost per share of a trade, measured by the bid–ask spread.
    • Quoted spread \(P_A - P_B\); proportional spread \((P_A - P_B)/P_M\); effective spread \(2\lvert P_t - P_M\rvert\).
    • Example: Axis Bank at 664.40 / 664.55 has a spread of 0.15, or 0.0226% of the mid.
    • Impact: a narrow spread lowers transaction costs and the cost of capital, and encourages trading.
  3. Depth: the quantity that can be traded near the best prices without moving them. Measured by \(\tfrac12(Q_A + Q_B)\).
    • Example: Axis Bank depth is 583.5 shares. A 2,000-share buy walks the book, averages 664.582 and pushes the ask up to 664.60.
    • Impact: depth decides the price impact of large or institutional orders. Depth and spread are negatively related (Chordia et al.: correlation −0.40).
  4. Resiliency: how quickly prices and the book return to fundamental levels after a large uninformed trade.
    • Example: new limit sellers restore the 664.55 ask within seconds of the buy.
    • Impact: high resiliency keeps transitory volatility short-lived and prices informative, which is good for price discovery.

Part (b) [3 marks]: the monopolistic market maker.

Answer: immediacy is the critical dimension.

  1. The context: Garman's (1976) dealership market. A single monopolistic dealer is the only counterparty. Public buy and sell orders arrive asynchronously, as independent Poisson flows \(\lambda_a(p_a)\) and \(\lambda_b(p_b)\). No buyer ever meets a seller directly.
  2. Why immediacy matters most there.
    • Because arrivals don't coincide, trade happens only if someone stands ready to take the other side now. The dealer does this from his own stock and cash, so every order is executed immediately against his inventory.
    • Immediacy is therefore the product he sells, and the spread \(p_a - p_b\) is its price. Garman's spread exists partly to pay for it and partly to cut the failure probability.
    • Width is just the price he charges, and he sets it himself as the monopolist, choosing the spread that maximises \(\pi = \bar s\,\bar\lambda\).
  3. Why inventory ties it together.
    • The dealer cannot borrow. If order flow runs out his cash or his stock (gambler's ruin), he fails, and the market loses all its immediacy at once.
    • So he sets \(\lambda_a p_a > \lambda_b p_b\) (cash inflow beats outflow) and keeps inventory drift at zero, \(\lambda_a = \lambda_b\). That way he can keep supplying immediacy indefinitely.
    • In short, depth (his inventory) is only the constraint. Immediacy is what the market needs from him.

Takeaway. Liquidity has four dimensions:

  • Immediacy: how fast
  • Width: at what cost, measured by the spread
  • Depth: how much, measured by \(\tfrac12(Q_A + Q_B)\)
  • Resiliency: how fast prices recover

For Garman's monopolist, immediacy is critical. Buy and sell orders arrive asynchronously, so the dealer is the only source of immediate execution. The spread is the price of that immediacy, and zero-drift inventory is how he keeps supplying it without failing.

Exam

How to adapt this to similar questions:

  • "Define liquidity and its dimensions" (2024 Q2, 5 = 2 + 3): use the definition plus the four-row table.
  • "Which dimension is critical for X": reason from who is missing in that market.
    • No counterparty present → immediacy.
    • Large orders → depth.
    • Many small trades → width.
    • After shocks or news → resiliency.

2.1 Harris's taxonomy (Intro p9). Every trader is either a principal (trades for their own account) or an agent (trades for someone else).

Buy side vs sell side (Exchanges p6–7). The buy side buys exchange services: investors, borrowers, hedgers, asset exchangers, gamblers. The sell side sells those services: dealers (supply liquidity) and brokers (arrange trades); broker-dealers do both.

2.2 Why they compete.

  • Trading is zero-sum relative to the market average (Exchanges p4). One trader's gain over the average is another's loss, so every trade has a winner and a loser.
  • Informed traders compete with each other for the same information. The first to trade on it captures the profit, and each one's trading moves the price and shrinks what is left for the rest.
  • Liquidity suppliers compete for order flow. Dealers and limit-order traders undercut each other's quotes (price priority) or arrive first (time priority), which narrows the spread.
  • Informed traders compete against dealers. Dealers lose to well-informed speculators because they end up on the wrong side of the trade (Exchanges p4), so dealers widen spreads to protect themselves.
  • Parasitic traders compete against large traders. Front-runners try to trade ahead of big orders.
  • Patient vs impatient. Patient traders get better prices because they search longer (Exchanges p4). Impatient traders pay for immediacy.

2.3 Madhavan (2000), from the paper. A survey of four areas: (1) price formation and price discovery; (2) market structure and design; (3) transparency; (4) the link between microstructure and other areas of finance. The points the question wants:

  • Prices form from the interaction of informed traders, uninformed (liquidity) traders and market makers. The market maker cannot tell them apart, so the spread must cover losses to the informed with gains from the uninformed (inventory and information models).
  • Uninformed traders are necessary. Without them the informed have no one to trade with profitably, and the market can break down (the no-trade result). Liquidity traders are the ones who "pay" for price discovery.
  • Informed traders make prices informative. Their trades push prices to fundamental value.
  • Market design changes who participates. Competition among dealers, transparency and fragmentation shift costs between trader groups. Transparency, for example, helps uninformed traders but can hurt dealers who hold large positions.

3 · Comprehensive solution

(a) Categories [3]. Following Harris's taxonomy:

  1. Utilitarian traders trade for a reason other than profit: investors and borrowers (move wealth through time), hedgers (cut risk), asset exchangers (swap assets), gamblers.
  2. Profit-motivated traders.
    • Informed speculators: value traders, news traders, arbitrageurs.
    • Parasitic speculators: order anticipators and front-runners, bluffers.
    • Dealers: market makers, specialists, block facilitators.
  3. Futile traders: pseudo-informed traders who lose on average.
  4. Agents: brokers and block brokers who trade for clients.

The buy side (investors, hedgers, borrowers) demands exchange services; the sell side (dealers, brokers) supplies them.

(b) Why they compete [2].

  • Relative to the market average, trading is a zero-sum game: profit for one trader means a loss for another.
  • Informed traders race each other to trade on the same information before the price absorbs it.
  • Dealers and limit-order traders compete on price and time priority for order flow, which narrows spreads.
  • Dealers try to avoid losing to informed traders by widening spreads, while parasitic traders try to profit from large traders' orders.

(c) Why each type matters to regulators and to the market [2].

  • Utilitarian and uninformed traders are the reason markets exist. They create the gains from trade and pay the spread that funds liquidity. Without them, informed traders have no counterparty and the market breaks down (Madhavan; Biais–Glosten–Spatt: informed profits are the mirror image of uninformed losses).
  • Informed traders make prices informative. They drive price discovery, and regulators want accurate prices for capital allocation.
  • Dealers supply liquidity and immediacy, and they bridge buyers and sellers who arrive at different times (Garman).
  • Arbitrageurs keep prices consistent across markets (Exchanges p5).
  • Brokers lower search costs.
  • What regulators watch for:
    • Balance. Too much informed trading raises adverse selection, which widens spreads and drives the uninformed away (Akerlof-type market failure).
    • Parasitic trading. Front-running and manipulation transfer wealth without adding information, so regulators police them to keep markets fair and orderly.

Takeaway. Categories: utilitarian, informed, parasitic, dealers, futile, agents (buy side vs sell side).

They compete because trading is zero-sum and speed and price priority decide who profits.

Every group has a job: the uninformed fund liquidity, the informed make prices right, dealers supply immediacy, arbitrageurs link markets. Regulators keep the mix balanced so that adverse selection and manipulation do not drive the uninformed out (Madhavan 2000).

Q3 · Liquidity, transaction costs and transitory volatility [6 = 2 + 2 + 1 + 1]

Question: What are the relationships among liquidity, transaction costs, and transitory volatility? To which volatility component do the price impacts of news traders contribute? To which volatility component do the price impacts of value traders contribute? What causes volatility to vary over time? How would you measure time-varying volatilities?

2 · Deep-dive concept summary

  • Fundamental volatility: price changes caused by unexpected changes in value, such as earnings surprises or central bank decisions. It is permanent and does not revert (p3, p10).
  • Transitory volatility: price changes caused by uninformed, impatient traders' demand for liquidity, such as bid–ask bounce or a large one-off trade. It is temporary: prices revert to fundamental value (p8–10).
  • Total volatility = fundamental + transitory (p10). Reversion makes successive price changes negatively correlated, so negative serial correlation is the signature of transitory volatility (p11–12).
  • The link to transaction costs is exact in Roll's model (p14). \[\text{Var}(\Delta P_t) = \underbrace{\sigma^2}_{\text{fundamental}} + \underbrace{\tfrac12 S^2}_{\text{transitory}}\] Here \(\sigma^2\) is the variance of the value change and \(S\) is the spread. The transitory part grows with the square of the spread, which is the transaction cost.
  • Informed vs uninformed trades.
    • Informed traders' trades move prices to the new value, so their price impact is fundamental (p4). Dealers' inference from order flow, the adverse-selection part of the spread, is also fundamental (p5).
    • Uninformed traders' impact is transitory. It is reversed when value traders or arbitrageurs notice the mispricing and trade against it (p9).
  • News traders vs value traders (Harris Ch 20).
    • News traders trade on new information (announcements, events) the moment it arrives.
    • Value traders estimate fundamental value and trade whenever the price is away from it. They buy after uninformed selling pushes the price down, and sell after uninformed buying pushes it up.

3 · Comprehensive solution

(a) Relationship [2].

  1. More liquidity means lower transaction costs. A deep, tight, resilient market has a small spread and small price impact per order.
  2. Lower transaction costs mean lower transitory volatility. Transitory volatility is the price moving away from value because of liquidity demand. In Roll's model it equals \(\tfrac12 S^2\), so a smaller spread gives less bid–ask bounce. In a deep book an uninformed order also moves the price less, and the move reverts faster.
  3. The chain is: illiquid market → high transaction costs → high transitory volatility. Liquidity suppliers earn their revenue by absorbing that transitory volatility, buying below value and selling above it.

(b) News traders [1 of the 2]. Their price impacts contribute to fundamental volatility. They trade on new information about value, so the prices they move do not revert: the move is permanent.

(b) Value traders [1 of the 2].

  • They trade against deviations caused by uninformed traders, and that pushes prices back towards fundamental value (slide p9).
  • The price change they cause is the reversal half of the transitory swing. So their impact belongs to transitory volatility, and it dampens it: the faster value traders act, the smaller and shorter the transitory component.
  • To the extent that their valuation reflects genuinely new information about value, that part of their impact is fundamental.

(c) Why volatility varies over time [1].

  • Fundamental side: information arrives unevenly (earnings seasons, central-bank meetings, crises); uncertainty about fundamentals rises and falls (for example tech stocks); political risk; changes in leverage (slides p6–7).
  • Transitory side: liquidity changes. Spreads widen and depth drops in stress, and liquidity moves together across stocks (Chordia et al. 2000: commonality). Order imbalances and uninformed trading pressure change over time, and volume and volatility both follow an intraday U-shape.
  • The result is volatility clustering: calm and turbulent periods each tend to persist.

(d) How to measure time-varying volatility [1].

  • Rolling-window standard deviation (or mean absolute deviation) of returns over the last \(k\) days. This is the slide measure, applied through time.
  • Realised volatility: the sum of squared intraday returns, which gives a fresh estimate each day.
  • GARCH(1,1): \(\sigma_t^2 = \omega + \alpha\,r_{t-1}^2 + \beta\,\sigma_{t-1}^2\), which models clustering directly.
  • Range-based estimators (Parkinson high–low), and implied volatility from option prices (for example India VIX).
  • To split the components: use Roll's estimator for the transitory part (\(\tfrac12 S^2\)), or Hasbrouck's (1993) VAR, which separates the permanent (fundamental) and transitory parts of price changes (slide p16).

Takeaway. Liquidity ↑ ⇒ transaction costs ↓ ⇒ transitory volatility ↓. In Roll's model the transitory part is exactly \(\tfrac12 S^2\).

News traders → fundamental (permanent). Value traders → they reverse transitory moves and so dampen transitory volatility.

Volatility varies with how fast information arrives and with liquidity conditions. Measure it with rolling standard deviations, realised volatility or GARCH, and decompose it with Roll or Hasbrouck's VAR.

Q4 · Garman (1976): bid for a target profit, spread, inventory [7 = 3 + 2 + 2]

Buy orders arrive as a Poisson process with rate \(\lambda_b = 12\)/hour; sell orders with rate \(\lambda_s = 8\)/hour. Each order is for 1 unit. The dealer's valuation is \(V = 100\). Following Garman's price setup, the dealer sets a bid \(P_b\) and an ask \(P_a\); the spread is \(S = P_a - P_b\). The dealer wants (1) expected inventory to be stable (arrival rates balanced) and (2) expected profit per unit time of at least 5 per hour.

Question: (a) If \(P_a = 101.5\), find the \(P_b\) needed for an expected profit of 5 per hour. (b) Compute the resulting spread and comment on it. (c) Comment on the dealer's inventory level based on the buy and sell arrival rates.

2 · Deep-dive concept summary

  • Setup (slides p10–11). One monopolist dealer. Buy and sell orders are independent Poisson flows whose rates depend on price: a higher ask means fewer buyers, a higher bid means more sellers. The dealer fixes one bid and one ask for all time, cannot borrow cash or stock, and fails if either runs out.
  • Inventory dynamics (p12): cash \(I_c(t) = I_c(0) + p_a N_{buy}(t) - p_b N_{sell}(t)\); stock \(I_s(t) = I_s(0) + N_{sell}(t) - N_{buy}(t)\).
    • Expected change in stock per hour = sell rate − buy rate.
    • Expected change in cash per hour = \(p_a\cdot\)buy rate − \(p_b\cdot\)sell rate.
  • Failure (gambler's ruin, p17–18):
    • Stock failure is certain unless the sell (stock-in) rate exceeds the buy (stock-out) rate.
    • Cash failure is certain unless cash inflow \(\lambda_a p_a\) exceeds cash outflow \(\lambda_b p_b\).
    • Avoiding both needs \(\lambda_a p_a > \lambda_b p_b\) and \(\lambda_b > \lambda_a\) (p19). The borderline case is zero drift, \(\lambda_a = \lambda_b\).
  • Profit (p20): with balanced flows at a common rate \(\bar\lambda\), every buy is matched by a sell. Each matched pair (a round trip) earns \(p_a - p_b = S\). So \[\bar\pi = S \times \bar\lambda .\]

3 · Comprehensive solution

Setup. Buy rate \(= 12\)/hr, sell rate \(= 8\)/hr, \(P_a = 101.5\), \(V = 100\), target \(\bar\pi = 5\)/hr. Balanced flow requires stock in = stock out.

(a) Required bid [3].

  1. Balanced rate: \(\bar\lambda = \min(12, 8) = 8\) round trips per hour.Why: for stable inventory, units sold must equal units bought. Only 8 sellers arrive per hour, so at most 8 of the 12 buyers can be served without draining stock (condition 1).
  2. Profit: \(\bar\pi = (P_a - P_b)\,\bar\lambda = 8\,(101.5 - P_b)\).Why: Garman \(\bar\pi = \bar s\bar\lambda\) (slide p20); each round trip earns the spread.
  3. Set \(8(101.5 - P_b) = 5\): \(101.5 - P_b = 0.625\), so \(P_b = \) 100.875.Why: condition 2 at equality (the lowest acceptable profit).
  4. Check against \(V\): profit = \(8(P_a - V) + 8(V - P_b) = 8(1.5) + 8(-0.875) = 12 - 7 = 5\). ✓Why: the gain over value on sales, plus the gain over value on purchases, adds up to the spread times the rate.

(b) Spread and comment [2].

  • \(S = 101.5 - 100.875 = \) 0.625, which is 0.625% of \(V\). This is the minimum spread that meets the profit target: \(S_{\min} = \bar\pi / \bar\lambda\). Any wider spread earns more.
  • Placement: both quotes are above \(V = 100\) (the mid is 101.19).
    • The dealer pays 100.875 for something he values at 100, so he loses 0.875 on every purchase, and makes it back by selling at 101.5.
    • Quoting high makes sense under excess buying pressure (12 buys vs 8 sells): a high ask discourages buyers, a high bid attracts sellers. This is the Amihud–Mendelson logic that low inventory raises both prices.
  • The spread is thin: it covers only the minimum profit, with no cushion for risk. Garman's point is that the spread also has to cut the failure probability.

(c) Inventory [2].

  • If the dealer fills every order at these fixed quotes, the stock drift is sell rate − buy rate \(= 8 - 12 = -4\) units per hour. Inventory falls on average by 4 units every hour.
  • By Garman (2.9), stock failure is certain: the buy (stock-out) rate 12 exceeds the sell (stock-in) rate 8. Starting with \(I_s(0)\) units, stock runs out after about \(I_s(0)/4\) hours on average.
  • Cash side: inflow \(12 \times 101.5 = 1218\) exceeds outflow \(8 \times 100.875 = 807\), so cash grows. The cash-failure probability is \((807/1218)^{I_c(0)/\bar p} < 1\).
  • Conclusion. The dealer is cash-rich and stock-poor, and condition 1 fails at the given arrival rates. To stabilise inventory he must raise \(P_a\) (fewer buys) and/or \(P_b\) (more sells) until the two rates are equal. Garman's fixed prices cannot do that midstream, which is exactly what Amihud–Mendelson later fix.

⚠ Ambiguity. The paper doesn't say which \(\bar\lambda\) to use. We used the balanced (matched) rate 8, which is the only rate consistent with stable inventory. If the examiner means the average rate \((12+8)/2 = 10\), then \(P_b = 101.5 - 5/10 = 101.0\) and \(S = 0.5\). State your assumption in the exam.

Takeaway. \(\bar\pi = S\bar\lambda\) with \(\bar\lambda = 8\) ⇒ \(S = 0.625\), \(P_b = 100.875\).

Both quotes sit above \(V\) because buying pressure is high.

At 12 buys vs 8 sells, inventory drifts down 4 units per hour, so stock failure is certain while cash builds up. The dealer must raise both quotes to rebalance.

Q5 · Amihud & Mendelson (1980): transaction rate and zero-drift bid [6 = 3 + 3]

Buy-order (demand) arrival rate at ask \(P_a\): \(D(P_a) = 100 - 2P_a\). Sell-order (supply) arrival rate at bid \(P_b\): \(S(P_b) = 20 + P_b\). Each trade is one unit. The total expected transaction rate is \(\lambda = D(P_a) + S(P_b)\). If \(D(P_a) = S(P_b) = \lambda/2\), inventory has zero drift.

2 · Deep-dive concept summary

  • Demand \(D(P_a)\) = public buy orders per unit time. It falls as the ask rises (\(D' = -2 < 0\)). At \(P_a = 50\), \(D = 0\): 50 is the choke price.
  • Supply \(S(P_b)\) = public sell orders per unit time. It rises with the bid (\(S' = +1 > 0\)).
  • Inventory drift = \(S(P_b) - D(P_a)\) units per unit time: sells add stock, buys remove it. Zero drift means \(D = S\).
  • Birth–death process (slide p27). The next order is a buy with probability \(D/(D+S)\), and the waiting time is exponential with mean \(1/(D+S)\). The dealer controls both rates through prices.
  • A–M finding (p31): bid and ask both decrease as inventory rises. With too much stock, the dealer lowers the ask to sell more and lowers the bid to buy less, which pulls inventory back to the preferred level. The spread stays positive.

3 · Comprehensive solution

(a) [3].

  1. \(D(50) = 100 - 2(50) = 0\) buy orders per unit time.Why: substitute \(P_a = 50\).
  2. \(S(48) = 20 + 48 = 68\) sell orders per unit time.Why: substitute \(P_b = 48\).
  3. \(\lambda = D + S = 0 + 68 = \) 68 trades per unit time.Why: definition of \(\lambda\).
  4. Comment. Every trade is a dealer purchase, so inventory drifts by \(+68\) per unit time and cash drains by \(68 \times 48 = 3264\) per unit time. These quotes are far from zero drift. The ask of 50 has priced out all buyers, and the high bid of 48 invites heavy selling. By A–M, the dealer should cut both prices.

(b) [3].

  1. \(D(30) = 100 - 2(30) = 40\).Why: substitute \(P_a = 30\).
  2. Zero drift: \(S(P_b) = D(P_a) \Rightarrow 20 + P_b = 40 \Rightarrow P_b = \) 20.Why: the zero-drift condition \(D = S\).
  3. Spread \(= P_a - P_b = 30 - 20 = \) 10.
  4. Check: \(\lambda = 40 + 40 = 80\), so \(D = S = \lambda/2 = 40\) ✓. Expected profit \(= 40(30) - 40(20) = 400\) per unit time.Why: cash in from 40 sales at 30, minus cash out for 40 purchases at 20.

Going further (Garman's monopoly optimum, slide p20). In slide notation: \(\lambda_S = \alpha + \beta p\) with \(\alpha = 20, \beta = 1\), and \(\lambda_B = \gamma - \delta p\) with \(\gamma = 100, \delta = 2\).

  • \(\bar\lambda = \dfrac{\gamma\beta + \alpha\delta}{2(\beta + \delta)} = \dfrac{100 + 40}{6} = 23.33\).
  • Bid \(= (\bar\lambda - \alpha)/\beta = 3.33\); ask \(= (\gamma - \bar\lambda)/\delta = 38.33\).
  • Spread \(= 35\), \(\bar\pi = 35 \times 23.33 = 816.7\).

A profit-maximising monopolist quotes a much wider spread and trades less. The quotes 30/20 in (b) give up profit for more volume.

(b) \(D(30) = 40 = S(P_b)\) ⇒ \(P_b = 20\), spread \(= 10\), \(\lambda = 80\).

2 · Deep-dive concept summary

Setup (p33). Dealers are liquidity suppliers with a utility function over terminal wealth (risk-averse). The model has two periods and one transaction per interval, with no bankruptcy. The dealer accepts a trade only if it leaves his expected utility unchanged: \(E[U(W_0(1+\tilde R^*))] = E[U(\tilde W)]\) (2.13).

The cost of a trade of size \(Q_i\), as a proportion (2.14 plus p41): \[c_i = \underbrace{\frac{z}{W_0}\sigma_{ip}Q_p + \frac12\frac{z}{W_0}\sigma_i^2 Q_i}_{\text{holding (inventory) cost}} + \underbrace{\gamma a_i}_{\text{information cost}} + \underbrace{\frac{M}{Q_i}}_{\text{order cost}}\]

  • \(z\) = the dealer's relative risk aversion (the Pratt index).
  • \(W_0\) = the dealer's initial wealth.
  • \(\sigma_i^2\) = the variance of stock \(i\)'s return.
  • \(\sigma_{ip}\) = the comovement of stock \(i\) with the dealer's existing portfolio.
  • \(Q_p\) = the dealer's existing inventory.
  • \(a_i\) = the expected return to informed traders; \(\gamma = +1\) if the dealer buys, −1 if he sells.
  • \(M\) = a fixed cost per order.

Spread (2.16 and p44). Take the cost of a purchase minus the cost of a sale, for equal sizes \(|Q|\). The \(Q_p\) term cancels, and \[s_i = \frac{z\sigma_i^2}{W_0}|Q_i| + 2a_i + \frac{2M}{|Q_i|}.\] It is linear in risk aversion, trade size and variance, and independent of existing inventory. Inventory shifts both prices but not their difference. As a function of \(|Q|\) it is U-shaped.

Competition (p43–45). Each dealer's average cost is lowest at \(Q_i^* = \sqrt{2MW_0/(z\sigma_i^2)}\). Here falling order cost per dollar is offset by rising holding cost; information cost is unaffected because it doesn't depend on size. With free entry, the number of dealers is \(d_i^* = |D_i|/|Q_i^*|\), the demand the market can support when every dealer operates at minimum average cost.

3 · Comprehensive solution

(a) Spread components [4].

  1. Holding (inventory) cost, \(\frac{z}{W_0}\sigma_{ip}Q_p + \frac12\frac{z}{W_0}\sigma_i^2 Q_i\): the risk premium a risk-averse dealer needs to hold an undiversified position. It rises with risk aversion \(z\), return variance \(\sigma_i^2\) and trade size \(Q_i\), and falls with wealth \(W_0\).
  2. Order-processing cost, \(M/Q_i\): a fixed cost per transaction (clearing, record-keeping, the dealer's time). Per dollar traded it falls as trade size grows.
  3. Information (adverse-selection) cost, \(\gamma a_i\): compensation for trading with better-informed investors. It is independent of trade size.
  4. Resulting spread: \(s_i = \dfrac{z\sigma_i^2}{W_0}|Q_i| + 2a_i + \dfrac{2M}{|Q_i|}\).
    • It is U-shaped in trade size and independent of current inventory \(Q_p\).
    • Inventory moves the placement of the bid and ask, through \(\sigma_{ip}Q_p\).

(b) Why the spread is not simply a transaction cost [part of 4].

  • The spread is the price of a service, immediacy, supplied by a risk-bearing dealer. It is not a mechanical fee.
  • Only \(M/Q\) is a real processing cost.
  • The holding-cost part pays for risk. It depends on the dealer's own risk aversion and wealth, and on the stock's variance and correlation with the dealer's portfolio. So two dealers with the same technology quote different spreads.
  • The information part is a transfer that recovers losses to informed traders. It is not a resource cost.
  • So the spread is an equilibrium price set by the supply of dealer services and the demand for immediacy, and it changes with market conditions (Stoll 2000: real vs informational friction).

(c) Competition among dealers [2].

  • With free entry, dealers enter until each operates at minimum average cost, trading \(Q^* = \sqrt{2MW_0/(z\sigma^2)}\). The market supports \(d^* = |D|/|Q^*|\) dealers.
  • So competition pushes the spread down to the minimum average cost of immediacy and removes monopoly rents. This narrows spreads, deepens liquidity and lowers the cost of capital.
  • More demand (\(|D|\)) or riskier stocks (larger \(\sigma^2\), smaller \(Q^*\)) support more dealers.
  • Evidence: with few dealers, prices are non-competitive. NASDAQ dealers colluded before 1994, and spreads fell sharply after the 1997 reforms let limit orders compete (Biais–Glosten–Spatt).
