  1. Quote vs trade. A quote is a price offered for trading: the bid (the highest price someone will pay) and the ask (the lowest price someone will sell at). A trade (transaction) happens at some price \(P_t\), where \(t = 1, 2, 3, \dots\) numbers the trades in order.
  2. Spread \(S\) = ask − bid. Mid = (bid + ask)/2. Example: bid 49.90, ask 50.10 → \(S = 0.20\), mid 50.00.
  3. Buyer- and seller-initiated trades. An impatient buyer takes the ask, so the trade prints at the ask. An impatient seller hits the bid, so it prints at the bid. \(Q_t = +1\) if trade \(t\) is a buy and \(-1\) if it is a sell (slide 13).
  4. Quoted vs effective spread. The quoted spread is ask − bid as posted. The effective spread is what traders actually pay: twice the distance between the trade price and the true value. They differ when trades happen inside the quotes.
  5. Fundamental value \(V_t\): what the stock is really worth at trade \(t\). The value innovation \(\varepsilon_t = V_t - V_{t-1}\) is the change in value caused by news.
  6. Random walk. \(V_t = V_{t-1} + \varepsilon_t\) with the \(\varepsilon_t\) independent over time, mean 0, variance \(\sigma^2\). Past changes tell you nothing about the next one.
  7. Efficient market. Prices (here, the values \(V_t\)) already reflect all information, so value changes cannot be predicted: exactly the random-walk property.
  8. Expected value, variance, covariance. \(E[X]\) = probability-weighted average. \(\text{Var}(X) = E[(X - EX)^2]\). \(\text{Cov}(X,Y) = E[(X - EX)(Y - EY)]\). If \(EX = EY = 0\), these simplify to \(\text{Var}(X) = E[X^2]\) and \(\text{Cov}(X,Y) = E[XY]\). Independent quantities have covariance 0. Covariance is bilinear: \(\text{Cov}(X_1 + X_2, Y) = \text{Cov}(X_1,Y) + \text{Cov}(X_2,Y)\), and constants factor out.
  9. Serial covariance (first-order autocovariance) of price changes: \(\text{Cov}(\Delta P_t, \Delta P_{t-1})\), the covariance of each price change with the one before it. Negative means a rise tends to be followed by a fall.
  10. Sample serial covariance from \(m\) pairs of consecutive changes \((x_i, y_i) = (\Delta P_t, \Delta P_{t-1})\): \(\widehat{\text{Cov}} = \frac1m\sum_i (x_i - \bar x)(y_i - \bar y)\), where \(\bar x, \bar y\) are the sample means.
  11. Bid–ask bounce (slide 8): trade prices jump between bid and ask as buys and sells alternate, even when the value does not move.
  12. Fundamental vs transitory volatility (slides 3–11). Fundamental volatility comes from news about value: it is permanent and does not revert. Transitory volatility comes from trading by uninformed, impatient traders (bounce, order imbalances): prices revert. Total = fundamental + transitory. Reversion makes consecutive price changes negatively correlated, so "negative serial correlation is a strong indicator of transitory volatility" (slide 11).

1 · Roll (1984): the spread hidden in price changes

Roll's serial covariance spread estimator

Only the sequence of trade prices \(P_1, P_2, \dots\) is observed: no quotes, and no record of which trades were buys. Each price equals the fundamental value plus or minus half the spread, depending on whether the trade was a buy or a sell.

Question: Estimate the effective spread \(S\) from trade prices alone, and split price-change variance into its fundamental and transitory parts.

Key idea

The spread leaves a fingerprint: a negative covariance between consecutive price changes. Each trade price is value \(\pm S/2\). Two consecutive price changes share the middle trade's \(\pm S/2\) term, once with a plus sign and once with a minus sign. That shared term makes their covariance exactly \(-S^2/4\), while news (a random walk) adds nothing to it.

Previous model → why it falls short

beforeRead the quoted spread (ask − bid) straight from the quote record.
assumesQuote data exists, and trades happen at the posted quotes.
fails atOld or thin markets with only closing or trade prices (e.g. daily CRSP returns); trades executed inside the quotes, so the quoted spread overstates the real cost.
thereforeInfer the effective spread from the trade-price series alone, using its serial covariance.

Solution

Setup (slide 13). \(P_t\) = price of trade \(t\); \(S\) = spread, constant; \(V_t\) = fundamental value at trade \(t\); \(\varepsilon_t = V_t - V_{t-1}\); \(Q_t = \pm1\) for a buy or a sell. Assumptions: (A1) \(P_t = V_t + \tfrac12SQ_t\) (slide 14). (A2) \(V\) is a random walk: the \(\varepsilon_t\) are independent over time with mean 0 and variance \(\sigma^2\). (A3) \(P(Q_t = +1) = \tfrac12\). (A4) \(Q_t\) is independent of all earlier \(Q\)'s. (A5) \(Q_t\) is independent of \(\varepsilon_t\) and \(\varepsilon_{t+1}\) (and of all other \(\varepsilon\)'s).

Claim. \(\text{Var}(\Delta P_t) = \sigma^2 + \tfrac12S^2\) and \(\text{Cov}(\Delta P_t, \Delta P_{t-1}) = -\tfrac14S^2\), so \(S = 2\sqrt{-\text{Cov}(\Delta P_t,\Delta P_{t-1})}\).

Part A: facts about \(Q\).

  1. \(E[Q_t] = \tfrac12(+1) + \tfrac12(-1) = 0\).Why: A3.
  2. \(Q_t^2 = 1\) always, so \(E[Q_t^2] = 1\).Why: \((+1)^2 = (-1)^2 = 1\).
  3. For \(s \ne t\): \(E[Q_tQ_s] = E[Q_t]E[Q_s] = 0\).Why: A4 (independence lets the expectation split), then step 1.

Part B: the price change.

  1. \(\Delta P_t = P_t - P_{t-1} = (V_t - V_{t-1}) + \tfrac12S(Q_t - Q_{t-1}) = \varepsilon_t + \tfrac12S\,\Delta Q_t\), where \(\Delta Q_t = Q_t - Q_{t-1}\).Why: subtract A1 at \(t-1\) from A1 at \(t\) (slide 14).
  2. \(\Delta Q_t\) takes the values \(-2, 0, +2\) with probabilities \(\tfrac14, \tfrac12, \tfrac14\). So the bounce alone moves the price by \(-S\), 0 or \(+S\).Why: the four equally likely pairs \((Q_{t-1}, Q_t)\) (A3, A4): \((+,+)\) and \((-,-)\) give 0; \((+,-)\) gives −2; \((-,+)\) gives +2.
  3. \(E[\Delta P_t] = E[\varepsilon_t] + \tfrac12S(E[Q_t] - E[Q_{t-1}]) = 0\).Why: A2 and step 1. So variance and covariance of \(\Delta P\) are plain expectations of squares and products (primer 8).

Part C: variance (slide 14).

  1. \(\text{Var}(\Delta P_t) = E[(\varepsilon_t + \tfrac12S\Delta Q_t)^2] = E[\varepsilon_t^2] + 2\cdot\tfrac12S\,E[\varepsilon_t\Delta Q_t] + \tfrac14S^2E[(\Delta Q_t)^2]\).Why: step 6, then expand \((x+y)^2 = x^2 + 2xy + y^2\).
  2. \(E[\varepsilon_t^2] = \sigma^2\) and \(E[\varepsilon_t\Delta Q_t] = E[\varepsilon_t]\,E[\Delta Q_t] = 0\).Why: A2 (mean 0, so \(E\varepsilon^2 = \sigma^2\)); A5 (independence splits the expectation).
  3. \(E[(\Delta Q_t)^2] = E[Q_t^2] - 2E[Q_tQ_{t-1}] + E[Q_{t-1}^2] = 1 - 0 + 1 = 2\).Why: expand the square; steps 2 and 3.
  4. \(\text{Var}(\Delta P_t) = \sigma^2 + \tfrac14S^2\cdot 2 = \sigma^2 + \tfrac12S^2\). The first term is fundamental volatility, the second transitory volatility.Why: steps 7–9; \(\sigma^2\) comes from news, \(\tfrac12S^2\) only from the spread (primer 12).

Part D: serial covariance (slide 15).

  1. \(\text{Cov}(\Delta P_t, \Delta P_{t-1}) = E\big[(\varepsilon_t + \tfrac12S\Delta Q_t)(\varepsilon_{t-1} + \tfrac12S\Delta Q_{t-1})\big]\) \(= E[\varepsilon_t\varepsilon_{t-1}] + \tfrac12S\,E[\varepsilon_t\Delta Q_{t-1}] + \tfrac12S\,E[\varepsilon_{t-1}\Delta Q_t] + \tfrac14S^2E[\Delta Q_t\Delta Q_{t-1}]\).Why: step 6 (both means are 0); multiply out the four products.
  2. The first three terms are 0.Why: \(\varepsilon_t\) and \(\varepsilon_{t-1}\) are independent with mean 0 (A2); every \(\varepsilon\) is independent of every \(Q\) with mean 0 (A5).
  3. \(E[\Delta Q_t\Delta Q_{t-1}] = E[(Q_t - Q_{t-1})(Q_{t-1} - Q_{t-2})] = E[Q_tQ_{t-1}] - E[Q_tQ_{t-2}] - E[Q_{t-1}^2] + E[Q_{t-1}Q_{t-2}] = 0 - 0 - 1 + 0 = -1\).Why: multiply out; step 3 for products of different \(Q\)'s, step 2 for \(Q_{t-1}^2\). Only the shared middle trade \(Q_{t-1}\) survives, and it enters once with each sign.
  4. \(\text{Cov}(\Delta P_t, \Delta P_{t-1}) = \tfrac14S^2\cdot(-1) = -\tfrac14S^2\).Why: steps 11–13.

Part E: the estimator and the volatility split.

  1. Solve step 14 for \(S\): \(S^2 = -4\,\text{Cov}\), and \(S = 2\sqrt{-\text{Cov}(\Delta P_t, \Delta P_{t-1})}\).Why: \(S > 0\), so take the positive root. This needs \(\text{Cov} < 0\).
  2. Roll's estimator replaces the true covariance with the sample serial covariance: \(\hat S = 2\sqrt{-\widehat{\text{Cov}}(\Delta P_t, \Delta P_{t-1})}\).Why: slide 15; the sample covariance (primer 10) estimates the true one.
  3. Fundamental variance: \(\hat\sigma^2 = \widehat{\text{Var}}(\Delta P) - \tfrac12\hat S^2 = \widehat{\text{Var}}(\Delta P) + 2\,\widehat{\text{Cov}}\).Why: step 10 solved for \(\sigma^2\); \(\tfrac12S^2 = \tfrac12\cdot(-4\,\text{Cov}) = -2\,\text{Cov}\).

Conclusion. In an efficient market with random, independent buys and sells, the effective spread is twice the square root of minus the first-order serial covariance of price changes. The same covariance splits total volatility into a fundamental part \(\sigma^2\) and a transitory part \(\tfrac12S^2\). ∎

Numerical example

(1) Why the average of \(\Delta Q_t\Delta Q_{t-1}\) is −1. List all 8 equally likely triples \((Q_{t-2}, Q_{t-1}, Q_t)\):

\(Q_{t-2}\)\(Q_{t-1}\)\(Q_t\)\(\Delta Q_{t-1}\)\(\Delta Q_t\)product

+1−1+1−2+2−4

−1+1+1+200
−1+1−1+2−2−4

Average \(= (-4 - 4)/8 = -1\), as in step 13. Only the two "zig-zag" paths (buy-sell-buy, sell-buy-sell) contribute, and both are reversals. That is bid–ask bounce.

(2) A ten-trade sample. The value is constant at 50.00 (\(\sigma^2 = 0\)) and the true spread is \(S = 0.20\), so buys print at 50.10 and sells at 49.90. The trades are B S S B S B B B B S.

  1. Prices: 50.10, 49.90, 49.90, 50.10, 49.90, 50.10, 50.10, 50.10, 50.10, 49.90.Why: A1 with \(V = 50\), \(S/2 = 0.10\).
  2. Nine changes \(\Delta P\): −0.2, 0, +0.2, −0.2, +0.2, 0, 0, 0, −0.2.Why: step 4; each is \(\pm S\) or 0 (step 5).
  3. Eight pairs (\(x = \Delta P_t\), \(y = \Delta P_{t-1}\)): (0, −0.2), (+0.2, 0), (−0.2, +0.2), (+0.2, −0.2), (0, +0.2), (0, 0), (0, 0), (−0.2, 0). Means: \(\bar x = 0\), \(\bar y = 0\).Why: the \(x\)'s sum to 0 and so do the \(y\)'s.
  4. Products \(xy\): 0, 0, −0.04, −0.04, 0, 0, 0, 0. \(\widehat{\text{Cov}} = -0.08/8 = -0.01\).Why: primer 10 with zero means.
  5. \(\hat S = 2\sqrt{0.01} = 2 \times 0.1 = 0.20\), the true spread.Why: step 16.

(3) A large simulation (200,000 trades, \(S = 0.10\), \(\sigma = 0.02\)): \(\widehat{\text{Cov}} = -0.002494\) against \(-S^2/4 = -0.0025\), \(\hat S = 0.0999\); \(\widehat{\text{Var}}(\Delta P) = 0.005407\) against \(\sigma^2 + S^2/2 = 0.0054\). If instead each trade repeats the previous direction with probability 0.8 (A4 broken), the same prices give \(\hat S = 0.040\): the estimator badly understates the spread.

(4) Volatility split. Suppose daily data give \(\widehat{\text{Var}}(\Delta P) = 0.0300\) and \(\widehat{\text{Cov}} = -0.0025\). Then \(\hat S = 2\sqrt{0.0025} = 0.10\), transitory variance \(= \tfrac12(0.10)^2 = 0.0050\), and fundamental variance \(= 0.0300 - 0.0050 = 0.0250\). So one-sixth of the variance is bounce.

Exam

Gotcha: in real samples \(\widehat{\text{Cov}}\) is often positive, and then \(\sqrt{-\widehat{\text{Cov}}}\) is undefined. Hasbrouck (2002) finds positive autocovariances for about half the stocks (Biais–Glosten–Spatt slides). Causes: small-sample noise; value changes that are not a random walk (A2 fails); quotes that move in the direction of trades because trades carry information. Clustered order flow (buys following buys, A4 fails) makes the estimate too small (simulation 3).

Limitations to list: spread assumed constant; trades assumed at the quotes (\(\pm S/2\)); buys and sells assumed equally likely and independent; no information in trades (no adverse selection), so it measures the order-processing, transitory part of the spread (Biais–Glosten–Spatt: "order-handling costs (Roll, 1984)"); needs an efficient market.

Recognize: "estimate the spread without quote data", "implicit / effective spread", "bid–ask bounce", "negative serial covariance of price changes", "transitory vs fundamental volatility" → Roll: \(S = 2\sqrt{-\text{Cov}}\), \(\text{Var} = \sigma^2 + S^2/2\).

Findings (paper abstract and the slides):

  • In an efficient market, the effective spread can be measured from the first-order serial covariance of price changes alone; no quote data is needed.
  • Roll computes the measure from daily and weekly returns of NYSE and AMEX stocks (1963–1982); the estimated spread is closely related to firm size (smaller firms have larger spreads).
  • Price-change variance = fundamental \(\sigma^2\) + transitory \(\tfrac12S^2\); negative serial covariance signals transitory volatility (slide 11).
  • Later work: Schultz (2000) applies Roll's method intraday; Hasbrouck (2004) estimates the Roll model with a Bayesian Gibbs sampler (Biais–Glosten–Spatt slide 7).
