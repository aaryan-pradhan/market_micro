Point of view. Every model is written from the dealer's side. The ask \(p_a\) is the price at which the dealer sells (a public buy). The bid \(p_b\) is the price at which the dealer buys (a public sell). Spread = ask − bid. A public sell raises the dealer's stock; a public buy lowers it.

Why inventory matters (slide 2): the dealer's wealth is limited; buys and sells arrive at different times, so he must hold inventory to bridge the gap; holding inventory costs money and carries risk.

1 · Garman (1976): fixed quotes and gambler's ruin

Setup

One monopolist dealer. Fixed \(p_a, p_b\). Public buys arrive as Poisson with rate \(\lambda_a(p_a)\) (falls as \(p_a\) rises), public sells arrive as Poisson with rate \(\lambda_b(p_b)\) (rises as \(p_b\) rises). He cannot borrow. Failure = cash hits 0 or stock hits 0.

\(I_s(t) = I_s(0) + N_S(t) - N_B(t)\) and \(I_c(t) = I_c(0) + p_a N_B(t) - p_b N_S(t)\).

Formulas

Stock failure: \(\;\left(\dfrac{\lambda_a}{\lambda_b}\right)^{I_s(0)}\) if \(\lambda_b > \lambda_a\), else \(1\).

Cash failure: \(\;\approx\left(\dfrac{\lambda_b p_b}{\lambda_a p_a}\right)^{I_c(0)/\bar p}\) if \(\lambda_a p_a > \lambda_b p_b\), else \(1\).

Survival needs both: \(\lambda_b > \lambda_a\) and \(\lambda_a p_a > \lambda_b p_b\). Together these give \(p_a > p_b\), so a positive spread is necessary to avoid certain failure.

Gambler's ruin (steps ±1, up with probability \(q\), \(\rho = (1-q)/q\)): \(P(\text{hit } 0 \mid \text{start } k) = \rho^k\) if \(\rho < 1\), and \(1\) if \(\rho \ge 1\).

Profit max with linear rates \(\lambda_b = \alpha + \beta p_b\), \(\lambda_a = \gamma - \delta p_a\), and a matched rate \(\lambda\):

\(\bar\lambda = \dfrac{\gamma\beta + \alpha\delta}{2(\beta+\delta)},\quad \bar p_b = \dfrac{\bar\lambda-\alpha}{\beta},\quad \bar p_a = \dfrac{\gamma-\bar\lambda}{\delta},\quad \bar\pi = (\bar p_a - \bar p_b)\,\bar\lambda\)

Numerical recipes

  • Poisson thinning: total rate \(\lambda\), buy with probability \(p\) ⇒ buys ~ Poisson(\(p\lambda\)), sells ~ Poisson(\((1-p)\lambda\)), independent. (2024: 5/min, 0.6 → 3 buys, 2 sells.)
  • Given \(n\) orders in total, buys ~ Binomial(\(n, p\)).
  • Profit = spread × matched rate \(\min(\lambda_a,\lambda_b)\), not × \((\lambda_a+\lambda_b)\). (2025: \(\bar\lambda = 8\), \(\pi = 5\) ⇒ \(S = 0.625\).)
  • Drift of stock = \(\lambda_b - \lambda_a\) per unit time. 12 buys vs 8 sells per hour ⇒ stock falls 4 per hour ⇒ certain stock failure, so raise both quotes.

Exam

Gotcha: zero drift (\(\lambda_a = \lambda_b\)) is not safe: \(\rho = 1\), so failure is certain. Even with a positive spread, failure has positive probability.

Findings: dealer's problem with asynchronous buy and sell orders; a dealer market is imperfect (inventory, not one clearing price, makes trade happen); inventory matters because running out means failure; the spread is partly there to cut failure probability, and reflects profit maximisation.

Recognize: "fixed bid and ask", "Poisson arrivals", "cannot borrow", "failure / ruin", "temporal microstructure".

2 · Amihud & Mendelson (1980): quotes depend on inventory

Setup

Garman's dealer, but at inventory level \(j\) he posts \(P_{aj}, P_{bj}\). Demand (buy) rate \(D(P_a)\), supply (sell) rate \(S(P_b)\). Inventory is bounded, \(0 \le j \le K\): a birth–death process. He maximises expected average profit per unit time, so he is risk-neutral.

Results

\(P_{bj} < \Delta_{j+1} < \Delta_j < P_{aj}\)

Here \(\Delta_j\) is the marginal worth of the \(j\)-th share. So the spread is positive at every level, and both quotes are decreasing in inventory. The drift \(\lambda_j - \mu_j\) is positive at \(j=0\) and negative at \(j=K\), so there is a preferred inventory position that the quotes push toward.

Numerical recipe

Zero drift ⇔ \(D(P_a) = S(P_b)\): solve for the missing price. Total rate \(= D(P_a) + S(P_b)\). (2025: \(D = 100 - 2P_a\), \(S = 20 + P_b\), \(P_a = 30\) ⇒ \(D = 40 = 20 + P_b\) ⇒ \(P_b = 20\), spread 10, rate 80.)

Exam

Gotcha: high inventory moves both quotes down; the spread does not just widen. The inventory effect comes from the inventory bounds, not from risk aversion (the slide saying "risk-averse" is wrong).

Findings: bid and ask monotone decreasing in inventory; there is a preferred inventory position; positive spread; the preferred position depends on order arrival rates, not on the asset's value.

Recognize: "prices depend on inventory", "birth–death", "preferred position", "extension of Garman".

3 · Stoll (1978): the risk-averse dealer's cost of immediacy

Setup

Two periods. The dealer maximises \(E\,U(\text{terminal wealth})\). He accepts a trade of size \(Q_i\) at cost \(C_i\) when it leaves expected utility unchanged: \(E\,U(\tilde W^*) = E\,U(\tilde W^* + \tilde X)\). Relative risk aversion \(z = -U''W_0/U'\), so \(z/W_0\) is the absolute risk aversion. \(Q_p\) = existing trading inventory. \(\sigma_{ip}\) = the covariance of stock \(i\) with that inventory. \(\sigma_i^2\) = variance of stock \(i\).

Formulas

Cost per rupee (2.14): \(c_i = \dfrac{z}{W_0}\sigma_{ip}Q_p + \dfrac12\dfrac{z}{W_0}\sigma_i^2 Q_i\) (\(Q_i > 0\) when the dealer buys, \(< 0\) when he sells).

Bid: \(\dfrac{P^* - P_b}{P^*} = c_i(+|Q|)\). Ask: \(\dfrac{P_a - P^*}{P^*} = -c_i(-|Q|)\).

Spread (2.16): \(s_i = \dfrac{z}{W_0}\sigma_i^2|Q|\), with no \(Q_p\) term.

With order cost \(M\) and information cost \(a_i\): \(s_i = \dfrac{z\sigma_i^2}{W_0}|Q| + 2a_i + \dfrac{2M}{|Q|}\) (holding + information + order). This is U-shaped in \(|Q|\).

Optimal scale: \(Q^* = \sqrt{\dfrac{2MW_0}{z\sigma_i^2}}\). Number of dealers: \(d^* = |D_i|/Q^*\).

Example

\(z/W_0 = 2\times10^{-6}\), \(\sigma_i^2 = 0.04\), \(|Q| = 50{,}000\), \(\sigma_{ip}Q_p = 0.02\times200{,}000\), \(P^* = 100\). Inventory term 0.008, size term 0.002 ⇒ bid 99.00, ask 99.40, spread 0.40. With \(Q_p = 0\): bid 99.80, ask 100.20, and the spread is still 0.40.

Exam

Gotcha: "independent of inventory" is true for the spread, not the quotes: \(Q_p\) shifts bid and ask together. Higher \(W_0\) lowers cost; higher \(z\) raises it. The spread is not just a transaction cost: it pays for risk bearing (holding), order processing and adverse selection (information). Competition among dealers drives the scale to \(Q^*\) and the number of dealers to \(d^*\).

Findings: cost = holding + order + information; spread linear in risk aversion, trade size and variance; independent of initial inventory; optimal scale \(Q^*\) and equilibrium number of dealers \(d^*\).

Recognize: "risk-averse dealer", "utility of terminal wealth", "two-period", "cost of immediacy", "number of dealers".

4 · Ho & Stoll (1981): multi-period, random orders and returns

Setup

Continuous time, horizon \(T\), time left \(\tau = T - t\). Orders arrive as Poisson with linear demand \(\lambda_a = \alpha - \beta a\) and \(\lambda_b = \alpha - \beta b\), where \(a, b\) are fees: ask \(= p + a\) and bid \(= p - b\). Inventory returns follow a Brownian motion with variance \(\sigma^2\). Wealth \(W = F\,(\text{cash}) + I\,(\text{inventory}) + Y\,(\text{base wealth})\). Absolute risk aversion \(R = -U''/U'\). Solved by dynamic programming; there is no closed form (Taylor series in \(\tau\)).

Formulas (first order in \(\tau\))

\(b^* = \dfrac{\alpha}{2\beta} + \dfrac{R\sigma^2\tau}{4}(2I + Q),\qquad a^* = \dfrac{\alpha}{2\beta} + \dfrac{R\sigma^2\tau}{4}(Q - 2I)\)

\(a^* + b^* = \underbrace{\dfrac{\alpha}{\beta}}_{\text{risk-neutral}} + \underbrace{\tfrac12 R\sigma^2 Q\tau}_{\text{risk adjustment}}\)

The \(\pm 2I\) cancel, so \(I\) is gone from the spread. Long inventory (\(I > 0\)) raises \(b^*\) and lowers \(a^*\), so both prices move down.

Example

\(\alpha/2\beta = 0.25\%\), \(R\sigma^2\tau/4 = 10^{-8}\), \(Q = 50{,}000\). At \(I = 0\): bid 99.70, ask 100.30, spread 0.60%. At \(I = 100{,}000\): bid 99.50, ask 100.10, spread still 0.60%. With \(\tau = 0.1\): spread 0.51%.

2024 Q3: bid 98, ask 100, holding 1,000 shares, sells 200 ⇒ spread profit \(200\times2 = \$400\), inventory 800, a $3 price drop costs \(800\times3 = \$2{,}400\). Inventory risk can dwarf spread revenue.

Exam

Gotcha: "the spread does not depend on inventory, but the dealer's price adjustment does". The risk part shrinks as \(\tau \to 0\), so the spread narrows toward the close.

Findings: the dealer's risk depends on the time horizon and falls as the end of trading nears; spread = risk-neutral spread + an uncertainty adjustment; the adjustment depends on transaction size, return variance and risk attitude; the spread is independent of inventory but placement is not; in an inactive stock with a minimum trade obligation, profit may not cover the risk; no closed form.

Recognize: "multi-period", "dynamic programming", "Poisson + Brownian / Wiener", "time horizon", "transaction and return uncertainty".

5 · O'Hara & Oldfield (1986): limit + market orders

Setup

Discrete time: \(n\) intervals per day, infinite horizon, objective \(E\sum_j \phi^j U(\text{daily profit})\). Buy orders \(= \alpha - a_n\gamma + \tilde\omega_n\) and sell orders \(= \beta + b_n\phi + \tilde\varepsilon_n\): the limit part is known, the market part (\(\tilde\omega, \tilde\varepsilon\)) is random. Inventory is carried overnight at repo rate \(r\), and its value is \(V(I_n)\).

Formulas

\(a_n = \dfrac{\alpha}{2\gamma} + \dfrac{E(U'\tilde\omega_n)}{2\gamma E(U')} + \dfrac{rE(U'\tilde p)}{2E(U')} + \dfrac{E(V')}{2E(U')}\)

\(b_n = -\dfrac{\beta}{2\phi} - \dfrac{E(U'\tilde\varepsilon_n)}{2\phi E(U')} + \dfrac{rE(U'\tilde p)}{2E(U')} + \dfrac{E(V')}{2E(U')}\)

\(a_n - b_n = \underbrace{\dfrac{\alpha\phi + \beta\gamma}{2\phi\gamma}}_{\text{known limit orders}} + \underbrace{\dfrac{\phi E\tilde\omega_n + \gamma E\tilde\varepsilon_n}{2\phi\gamma}}_{\text{risk-neutral (expected market orders)}} + \underbrace{\dfrac{\phi\,\text{cov}(U',\tilde\omega_n) + \gamma\,\text{cov}(U',\tilde\varepsilon_n)}{2\phi\gamma E(U')}}_{\text{risk adjustment}}\)

Key tool: \(E(U'X) = E(U')E(X) + \text{cov}(U', X)\). Risk-neutral ⇒ \(U'\) constant ⇒ covariances are 0.

Exam

Gotcha: the risk adjustment can have either sign, so the risk-averse spread can be smaller than the risk-neutral one. The inventory terms cancel out of \(a_n - b_n\), yet inventory still changes the spread through \(U'\) inside the covariances. So inventory affects both size and placement.

Findings: spread = known limit orders + risk-neutral adjustment for expected market orders + risk adjustment for market-order and inventory-value uncertainty; risk aversion affects both the spread and the individual quotes; inventory has a pervasive effect; new features: limit and market orders, discrete multi-period, daily payoff, no liquidation date, no fixed stock value.

Recognize: "limit and market orders", "discrete time, infinite horizon", "overnight repo", "three components of the spread".

6 · Roll (1984): the spread hidden in price changes

Setup

(A1) \(P_t = V_t + \tfrac12 S\,Q_t\) with \(Q_t = \pm1\) (buy / sell). (A2) \(V_t = V_{t-1} + \varepsilon_t\): a random walk, so the market is efficient, with variance \(\sigma^2\). (A3) Buy or sell with probability ½ each. (A4) Different trades are independent. (A5) \(\varepsilon\) is independent of \(Q\).

Formulas

\(\Delta P_t = \varepsilon_t + \tfrac12 S(Q_t - Q_{t-1})\)

\(\text{Var}(\Delta P) = \underbrace{\sigma^2}_{\text{fundamental}} + \underbrace{\tfrac12 S^2}_{\text{transitory}},\qquad \text{Cov}(\Delta P_t, \Delta P_{t-1}) = -\tfrac14 S^2\)

\(S = 2\sqrt{-\text{Cov}(\Delta P_t, \Delta P_{t-1})},\qquad \hat\sigma^2 = \widehat{\text{Var}}(\Delta P) + 2\,\widehat{\text{Cov}}\)

Example

\(V = 50\), \(S = 0.20\). Nine price changes: −0.2, 0, +0.2, −0.2, +0.2, 0, 0, 0, −0.2 ⇒ \(\widehat{\text{Cov}} = -0.08/8 = -0.01\) ⇒ \(\hat S = 2\sqrt{0.01} = 0.20\).

Volatility links

  • Fundamental (permanent) volatility comes from news: \(\sigma^2\). Transitory volatility comes from trading frictions: \(\tfrac12 S^2\). A negative serial covariance is the sign of transitory volatility.
  • Liquidity ↑ ⇒ transaction costs ↓ ⇒ transitory volatility ↓.
  • News traders' price impacts add to fundamental volatility. Value traders reverse transitory moves, so they reduce transitory volatility.

Exam

Gotcha / limitations: the sample covariance is often positive, and then \(\sqrt{-\text{Cov}}\) is undefined (Hasbrouck finds this for about half of stocks). Causes: small samples; \(V\) not a random walk; quotes that move with informed order flow; clustered orders (buys following buys), which bias \(\hat S\) down.

Findings: the effective spread can be measured from the serial covariance of price changes alone, with no quote data; it is estimated on NYSE and AMEX daily and weekly returns for 1963–1982; the estimated spread is strongly related to firm size (smaller firms have bigger spreads).

Recognize: "spread without quote data", "implicit / effective spread", "bid–ask bounce", "negative serial covariance".

7 · Chordia, Roll & Subrahmanyam (2000): commonality in liquidity

Five measures (averaged over a stock's trades each day)

MeasureFormulaUnit
QSPR (quoted)\(P_A - P_B\)$
PQSPR\((P_A - P_B)/P_M\)fraction of mid
DEP (depth)\(\tfrac12(Q_A + Q_B)\)shares
ESPR (effective)\(2\,|P_t - P_M|\)$
PESPR\(2\,|P_t - P_M|/P_t\)fraction of trade price

Example: bid 49.90 (600 shares), ask 50.10 (1,000 shares), trade at 50.08 ⇒ QSPR 0.20, PQSPR 0.40%, DEP 800, ESPR 0.16, PESPR 0.32%.

Spreads measure width; DEP measures depth. The other two dimensions of liquidity are immediacy and resiliency.

Commonality regression

\(DL_{j,t} = \alpha_j + \beta_j\,DL_{M,t} + (\text{lag and lead of } DL_M) + \text{controls} + e_{j,t}\)

\(DL_t = (L_t - L_{t-1})/L_{t-1}\) is the proportional daily change. \(DL_M\) is the equal-weighted average over all stocks except \(j\). Run one time-series regression per stock, then report the cross-sectional mean of the \(\beta_j\) with \(t = \bar\beta/(sd/\sqrt N)\). \(\beta_j = \sum(x-\bar x)(y-\bar y)/\sum(x-\bar x)^2\).

Result: mean concurrent \(\beta\) for DQSPR is 0.690 (t = 28.3), for DPQSPR 0.791. Lag and lead coefficients are small. \(R^2\) is small (about 0.01–0.02) but significant.

Exam

Gotcha: the regression uses changes, not levels. Stock \(j\) is left out of the market average. A low \(R^2\) does not mean no commonality. A stock's own number of trades raises its spread (information risk); market trading volume lowers it (inventory risk).

Findings: spreads and depth co-move with market-wide and industry-wide liquidity; the common part is small but significant, so liquidity is a systematic, non-diversifiable risk; later work links commonality to correlated institutional trading, investor sentiment and funding constraints, and finds it in 40 markets (Karolyi et al. 2012).

Recognize: "commonality", "co-movement of spreads across stocks", "market-wide liquidity factor".

8 · Amihud (2002): ILLIQ and stock returns

Formula

\(\text{ILLIQ}_{iy} = \dfrac{1}{D_{iy}}\sum_{d=1}^{D_{iy}} \dfrac{|R_{iyd}|}{\text{VOLD}_{iyd}}\;(\times 10^6)\)

This is the average absolute return per dollar traded, i.e. the price move per $1 million traded. Higher ILLIQ means less liquid. Compare the Amivest ratio \(\sum\text{VOLD}/\sum|R|\), where higher means more liquid.

Example: \(|R| = 2\%\) on $2M of volume ⇒ \(10^{-8}\) per dollar, 0.01 after ×10⁶. With 100 times the volume, ILLIQ is 100 times smaller.

Validity: \(\text{ILLIQ} = -292 + 247.9\lambda + 49.2\psi\), with \(R^2 = 0.30\). It rises with Kyle's \(\lambda\) (price impact) and with \(\psi\) (fixed cost).

Results

  • Cross-section (Fama–MacBeth, NYSE 1963–1997): expected return rises with ILLIQ, even after controlling for beta and size. This is the illiquidity premium. Characteristics are taken from the previous year.
  • Time series: expected market illiquidity has a + coefficient on future excess returns. Unexpected illiquidity has a − coefficient on same-period returns: the required return \(r\) rises, so the price \(P = D/r\) falls now.
  • Both effects are stronger for small stocks ("flight to liquidity"). The equity premium partly pays for illiquidity.

Exam

Gotcha: ILLIQ measures illiquidity, and Amivest measures liquidity. An unexpected rise in illiquidity means lower returns now, not higher.

Recognize: "\(|R|/\text{VOLD}\)", "price impact from daily data", "illiquidity premium", "expected vs unexpected illiquidity".
